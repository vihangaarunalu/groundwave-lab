        Concepts behind the lab

        Short explanations of each effect you can test. Open a card, then try the matching experiment in the Lab.

        1. What a ground wave is

        A ground (surface) wave is a vertically polarized wave that is guided along the boundary between air and earth. Currents induced in the ground drag the lower part of the wavefront, so it follows the earth's curvature well beyond the optical horizon. That is why MW (medium-wave) AM broadcasting gives stable daytime coverage over hundreds of kilometres.

        Horizontal polarization is short-circuited by the conducting ground, so groundwave systems use vertical masts.

        2. Ground constants and the Norton numerical distance

        Losses depend on ground conductivity σ and relative permittivity εr. The lab computes ITU-R P.368 the way the ITU's GRWAVE program does: the exact Sommerfeld–Norton solution for a flat earth, then the Fock/Wait residue series for the curved earth (see the Models tab for every step).

        εc = εr − j60λσ   Δ = √(εc − 1)/εc   w = −j(kd/2)Δ²  (|w| ≈ Norton's numerical distance p)
        F = 1 − j√(πw)·e−w·erfc(j√w)    E = 300·√P · |W| / d  [mV/m, kW, km]
        Quick hand estimate (Terman): A ≈ (2 + 0.3p)/(2 + p + 0.6p²), p = (π d / λ x)·cos b, x = 1.8×10⁴σ/fMHz

        Small p means little loss (sea water, low frequency). Large p means rapid attenuation (dry ground, high frequency).

        3. Why lower frequencies travel further

        p grows with frequency through both λ and x. At 200 kHz over sea the attenuation factor stays near 1 for hundreds of kilometres. At 10 MHz over dry land, groundwave is gone within a few tens of kilometres. This is why LF and MF carry broadcasting and navigation, and HF relies on the sky wave.

        4. Wave tilt

        Ground losses tilt the wavefront forward. The power flow then has a downward component that feeds the losses in the earth. The tilt angle is roughly tan ψ ≈ 1/√|εr − j x|. Wave tilt is larger over poor ground. The scene exaggerates it three times so you can see it.

        5. Earth curvature and refraction

        The flat-earth formula is good up to about 80/∛fMHz km. Beyond that, diffraction around the curved earth adds loss, roughly 0.04·∛f dB per km at standard refraction. Refraction in the lower atmosphere bends waves slightly. It is modelled with an effective earth radius factor k. Larger k (super-refraction in warm, humid inversions) reduces the curvature loss. k = 1 increases it.

        6. Sky wave and the ionosphere
        • D layer (60–90 km): exists in daylight only and absorbs LF/MF/low HF. The absorption scales roughly as 1/(f + fH)².
        • E layer (~110 km): reflects MF by day; it is weak at night.
        • F2 layer (250–350 km): reflects HF. Its critical frequency rises with solar activity.

        A wave returns when f ≤ fo·sec φ (secant law). Steep rays at high frequency escape, which creates the skip zone.

        7. Day, night and the fading zone

        By day, the D layer absorbs the MW sky wave, so only the ground wave is heard. At night the D layer vanishes and the sky wave returns strongly hundreds of kilometres away. Where ground and sky waves arrive with similar strength (within about 6 dB), their changing phase causes deep fading and distortion. The lab shades that zone on the plot.

        8. How weather enters the picture
        • Rain and monsoon raise soil conductivity and permittivity, so the ground wave reaches further.
        • Drought and frozen ground lower conductivity and shorten coverage.
        • Thunderstorms add atmospheric noise (static), so a stronger signal is needed for the same service. Night-time static travels far by sky wave too.
        • Rain attenuation in the air is negligible below a few GHz, so the lab ignores it.
        • Space weather controls the sky wave. Solar flares cause sudden HF fade-outs, and geomagnetic storms depress the F2 layer.
        9. Mixed paths (Millington's method)

        For a land–sea path, the field is the average of forward and reverse calculations, in dB:

        E = ½[ (E₁(d₁) − E₂(d₁) + E₂(d)) + (E₂(d₂) − E₁(d₂) + E₁(d)) ]

        Over sea after a coastline, the field can actually rise for a while. This is the "recovery effect".

        10. Limits of this lab

        The lab is a teaching model, not a planning tool. Use ITU-R P.368 (groundwave curves), P.527 (ground constants), P.1147 (MF sky wave), P.533 (HF) and P.372 (radio noise) for real work. The sky wave here is a median value. Real signals fade by 5–10 dB within minutes.

        Build and simulate your own model

        1. Lab → + New (or + Copy selected) creates a model. Click its name to rename it.
        2. Under Signal, choose the mechanism (ground wave, sky wave, both, or your own formula), then set the frequency, power and antenna heights.
        3. Under Ground & path and Time & weather, describe the path and the conditions. Or build a real path on the Map and choose Open in Lab.
        4. Read the result card and the plot, then open Animation to watch it propagate. Drag the receiver and use for full screen.
        5. To compare, copy the model and change exactly one setting.

        Glossary

        Ground (surface) waveVertically polarized wave guided along the earth's surface. Main MW/LF daytime service.
        Sky waveWave reflected (refracted) back to earth by the ionosphere.
        Conductivity σHow easily the ground carries current, in S/m. Sea ≈ 5, average land ≈ 0.003, rock ≈ 0.0001.
        Permittivity εrHow strongly the ground stores electric field energy. Matters most at higher frequencies.
        Numerical distance pNorton's measure of how "electrically far" a point is over lossy ground. Larger p means more loss.
        dBµV/mField strength in decibels above 1 µV/m. 60 dBµV/m = 1 mV/m. Every 20 dB is ×10.
        Critical frequency foHighest frequency a layer reflects at vertical incidence (foE, foF2).
        Skip zoneRegion with no ground wave and no returning sky wave.
        Fading zoneRegion where ground and sky waves are similar in strength and interfere.
        Millington's methodStandard way to combine different ground types along one path.
        Great-circle pathShortest route between two points on the earth's surface.

        Welcome to the Groundwave Propagation Lab

        This lab predicts how far a radio signal reaches, from LF and AM broadcasting up to VHF/UHF. You can change the ground, weather, time of day and terrain.

        1. Lab: pick a ready-made experiment, then change one setting and compare the curves.
        2. Animation: watch ground and sky waves travel. Use Quick settings to switch frequency, and for full screen.
        3. Map: place transmitters and receivers anywhere in the world. Each site gets a reception verdict.
        4. Terrain profile: see the mountains, buildings and main obstacle between a transmitter and a receiver.
        5. Plan a cell site (engineers): on the Map, load real cells (OpenCelliD), turn on the Coverage map for 2G/3G/4G/5G and look for red gaps. Add a candidate with + Transmitter, then set its networks, height, 3-sector azimuth and tilt in the Transmitters table. Watch "covered in view %" change.
        6. Rename pins: new pins are named from OpenStreetMap automatically. Select a pin and choose Rename (or press F2).
        7. Mobile coverage at home: on the Map, set a receiver at your home (Move RX or Use my location), then choose Find nearest cell towers. Towers mapped in OpenStreetMap are added as transmitters with their heights, ready to simulate 2G–5G.
        8. Learn and Quiz: read the theory and check your understanding.
        1. Start a model. In Lab → Models, choose + Add blank model, or select a model and choose + Copy selected. Rename it by clicking its name.
        2. Choose what to calculate. Under Settings → Signal, pick Ground wave + sky wave (most realistic), Ground wave only, Sky wave only, or My own formula.
        3. Set the station. Set the frequency (buttons or slider), transmitter power and antenna heights.
        4. Set the path. Under Ground & path, choose the ground type or a land-to-sea boundary. For a real path, place the sites on the Map and choose Open in Lab.
        5. Set conditions. Under Time & weather and Advanced, set the time of day, weather, solar activity and the listener's surroundings.
        6. Simulate. Read the result card and the plot. Choose Animation to watch it, and drag the receiver to any distance.
        7. Compare. Copy the model, change one setting, and compare the two curves. The table under Technical details lists both models side by side.

        Your own formula: choose My own formula and type the field strength E in mV/m. You can use d (km), f (MHz), P (kW), sigma, er, A (Norton attenuation factor) and lam (km). For example:

        300*sqrt(P)/d * exp(-0.004*d*pow(f,0.7))

        Plot it next to the built-in Ground wave only model to test your formula.

        Who it is for: engineering students, broadcast trainees and educators. Radio amateurs and engineers can also use it for quick what-if comparisons.

        Models used:

        • Ground wave: ITU-R P.368 by the GRWAVE method (exact Sommerfeld–Norton flat earth, Fock/Wait residue series for the curved earth) and the P.368 Millington method for mixed paths.
        • Sky wave: ITU-R P.1147 for LF/MF. HF uses a P.533-style teaching approximation (secant law, D-layer absorption); full P.533 needs the P.1239 ionospheric maps.
        • VHF/UHF: ITU-R P.1812 (free space, delta-Bullington diffraction, troposcatter) with P.2108 receiver clutter and P.1812/P.1546 location variability. Ducting at 50% time is not included.
        • Mobile networks: ITU-R P.529 Okumura–Hata and COST-231 Hata within 100 km, ITU-R M.2412 UMa within 5 km, ITU-R P.1812 beyond; P.838 rain, P.676 gases (approx.), P.2109 building entry.
        • Reception levels: ITU-R BS.703 (LF/MF/HF), BS.412 (FM), BT.417 (TV bands). Solar flares: NOAA D-RAP for HF absorption and a radio-burst noise model for VHF and above.
        • Terrain: Copernicus DEM GLO-90. VHF and above use the ITU-R P.1812 delta-Bullington method (P.526-15 §4.5) over the real profile, with OpenStreetMap buildings as surface heights. LF–HF ground waves (P.368) assume a smooth earth; the app adds a P.526 single knife-edge estimate for hills.

        Accuracy: the Models tab lists every equation, the checks that pass or fail for your inputs, built-in verification tests, and a tool to compare predictions with your own measurements. Use certified planning software for licensing or frequency coordination.
